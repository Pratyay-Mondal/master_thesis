## 1 Methodology

This cross-walk matrix systematically maps each sub-obligation of Article 50 of the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) to two evaluated technical provenance mechanisms: **C2PA** (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity), a hard-bound, container-level image provenance standard, and **SynthID-Text**, a statistical text watermarking scheme based on keyed pseudo-random functions. For each obligation–mechanism pair, the matrix assesses fulfilment status at three stages: (i) at the point of generation, (ii) after distribution through Meta's platform pipelines (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Threads), and (iii) at end-user consumption. Fulfilment is graded as fully satisfied (✅), conditionally or partially satisfied (⚠️), or not satisfied (❌). The "Meta Platform Gap" column documents specific failure modes discovered during empirical testing in Weeks 2–3, including the compression survival experiment (Week 2, \(n = 8\) pipeline configurations, 0% C2PA survival) and the text watermark robustness battery (Week 3, z-score degradation across six transformation classes). This approach ensures that the subsequent gap analysis and tool-design requirements are grounded in observed platform behaviour rather than theoretical specification alone.

## 3 Gap Analysis Summary

### 3.1 The Distribution-Channel Mismatch

Article 50 implicitly assumes that provenance signals embedded at the point of generation will survive intact through distribution channels and remain available for verification at the point of consumption. This assumption does not hold on Meta's social media platforms. The Week 2 compression survival experiment demonstrated that C2PA manifests achieve a 0% survival rate across all eight tested Meta pipeline configurations (Facebook feed upload, Instagram story, WhatsApp image share, Threads post, and their respective re-sharing paths). Server-side re-encoding — including JPEG quality reduction, resolution downscaling, and WebP conversion — invalidates the hard-bound cryptographic hash that binds the manifest to the image payload, and the JUMBF metadata container is physically excised during CDN ingest. The result is a fundamental structural mismatch: the legislative framework mandates machine-readable marking at distribution, but the dominant distribution infrastructure systematically destroys the mandated marks.

### 3.2 The Interoperability–Robustness Trade-Off

The two evaluated mechanisms exhibit complementary but mutually exclusive strengths. C2PA is fully interoperable — any compliant verifier can validate any provider's manifest using the open trust-list infrastructure — but it is maximally fragile, failing upon any byte-level modification to the signed payload. SynthID-Text, conversely, exhibits meaningful robustness to verbatim copy-paste and light lexical editing, but it is siloed per provider: each provider's keyed PRF uses a proprietary secret, and no federated key-exchange or cross-provider verification protocol has been standardised. Article 50(2) demands mechanisms that are simultaneously "effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable," yet no single deployed technology satisfies all four adjectives. This suggests that compliance may require a layered architecture combining multiple complementary mechanisms rather than reliance on any single standard.

### 3.3 The User-Interface Gap

Article 50(5) imposes a visibility requirement: provenance information must be presented "clearly and distinguishably" at the latest at first exposure. Both C2PA and SynthID-Text are, by design or by platform omission, invisible to end users on Meta platforms. C2PA defines a credential-rendering UI specification (the `cr` icon and provenance popover), but no Meta surface implements it. SynthID-Text is intentionally imperceptible — visibility would compromise text quality and reveal the watermarking scheme's statistical footprint. Consequently, neither mechanism can independently satisfy Art. 50(5). Compliance requires an additional presentation layer that translates machine-readable provenance signals into human-readable, visually salient disclosures at the point of content consumption.

### 3.4 The Complementary-Tool Requirement

The gap analysis converges on a clear architectural implication: satisfying Art. 50 on social media platforms requires a **complementary, multi-layered approach** combining (a) surviving technical marks — specifically, invisible perceptual image watermarks (e.g., frequency-domain embeddings robust to re-encoding) and statistical text watermarks for sufficiently long text spans — to provide machine-readable evidence that persists through platform transformations; (b) visible deployer labels, whether platform-native (Meta's "Made with AI") or regulation-mandated, to address the disclosure requirements of Art. 50(4); and (c) a client-side verification overlay — such as a browser extension — that queries available detection endpoints, aggregates multi-signal evidence, and renders Art. 50(5)-compliant visual disclosures to end users at first exposure. No single mechanism in isolation is sufficient; only their combination can approximate the compliance posture that Art. 50 envisions.

## 4 Implications for Tool Design

The gaps identified in Sections 2–3 translate directly into functional requirements for the browser-extension prototype to be developed in Months 2–3:

- **FR-01 — Image Provenance Fallback.** The extension must detect C2PA manifests when present but fall back to invisible perceptual watermark detection (e.g., frequency-domain or learned-embedding detectors) when C2PA metadata has been stripped by platform re-encoding. The fallback detector must tolerate JPEG quality factors as low as \(q = 40\) and resolution reductions of up to 50%.

- **FR-02 — Graceful Short-Text Handling.** The text watermark detection module must report an explicit "insufficient signal" status for text spans below the minimum reliable detection length (empirically, \(T < 50\) tokens, corresponding to \(z < 3\)) rather than returning a false negative. This prevents users from incorrectly concluding that short AI-generated text is human-authored.

- **FR-03 — Compliance Mapping Engine.** The extension must include a rule engine that maps detection outputs (C2PA validation result, watermark z-score, platform label presence) to Art. 50 obligation statuses (e.g., "Art. 50(2) — machine-readable marking: partially fulfilled via watermark; C2PA absent"). This mapping must be configurable to accommodate delegated-act specifications as they are published.

- **FR-04 — Art. 50(5)-Compliant UI Overlay.** The extension must render a visible, clearly distinguishable provenance indicator at the point of first content exposure — i.e., inline with the content in the social media feed, not behind a menu or secondary interaction. The indicator must be comprehensible to non-expert users while providing a drill-down path to full technical provenance details for expert verification.
